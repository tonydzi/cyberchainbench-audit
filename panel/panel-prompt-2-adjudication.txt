Hostile adjudication. Two positions are on the table. Tell me who is right and where BOTH are wrong.
Be concrete and brief. Do not flatter either side.

PAPER: "CyberChainBench" (arXiv 2606.26216). Headline: "a clear difficulty gradient: 37.5%
detection, 43.7% exploitation, but only 23.4% patching", concluding that producing a correct
fix is far harder than finding or reproducing a vulnerability.

VERIFIED from their own tables:
- Exploit metric R = min(observed_profit/reference_profit, 1.0): CONTINUOUS partial credit,
  best-of-N inside a 30-min budget, n=200 cases.
- Patch metric R = 1[exploit blocked] * 1[normal txs pass]: BINARY conjunction, n=94 cases,
  a different and smaller subset (filtered from 153 proxy-upgradeable cases).
- They also report patching as "Block / Pass %": e.g. GPT-5.5 = 47.9 / 23.4;
  Gemini 3.1 Pro = 98.9 / 1.1.

MY ORIGINAL CLAIM (call it A): the gradient is a measurement artifact; compared on the single
predicate "did the agent neutralize the vulnerability" (= block rate), patching is not harder
than exploiting, so the paper really measured "not breaking the protocol is hard", a regression
property rather than a security-reasoning property.

REBUTTAL I RECEIVED (call it B): A is invalid, because block rate is a degenerate metric with a
zero-cost trivial solution - a script appending revert() to every entry point scores ~100% block
rate with zero security reasoning. Gemini's 98.9% block / 1.1% pass is exactly that bricking
bias. Therefore the conjunction 1[blocked]*1[normal passes] is the MINIMUM VIABLE definition of
a patch, and decoupling the conjuncts is just excuse-making for models that emit broken code.

MY REFINED POSITION (call it C) after that rebuttal: I concede B on the degenerate baseline, and
I concede the paper's qualitative ordering (patching hardest) stands. But I claim a narrower
thing survives: the specific numbers 43.7 vs 23.4 are not comparable as a ratio of capability,
because they differ simultaneously in (i) credit model - a mean of a continuous score vs a mean
of a binary conjunction, (ii) sample - 200 vs 94 non-identical cases, (iii) effectively the
number of predicates that must hold at once. A mean of a continuous variable and a mean of a
0/1 conjunction are different estimands; you cannot read "exploitation is ~1.9x easier" off
them. The honest apples-to-apples comparison is still missing from the paper, and it is NOT
block rate - it is (1) binarize the exploit score at a profit threshold, e.g. R >= 0.9, and
(2) evaluate both tasks on the SAME 94 contracts, and (3) report trivial-baseline calibration
(the revert() script) so the patch floor is visible.
